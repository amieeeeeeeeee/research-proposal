Chapter I

The Puzzle

The old story

Disney princesses have long offered ideas about beauty, romance, marriage, femininity, and independence. But the princess has changed — from Cinderella and Sleeping Beauty, to Mulan, Rapunzel, Elsa, and Moana.

If the stories changed, did the meanings young women took from them change too?

Litt (2019) traces how Disney's stories and products in the 1950s tied gender together with romance, family, and growing up. At the same time, England, Descartes & Collier-Meek (2011) found that gender portrayals became more complex over time, even though the movement toward equality was not a straight line. So the puzzle isn't simply "are Disney princesses feminist?" It's: how do the people who grew up with these stories understand them now?

Chapter II

The Research Question

How do young women reflect on the ways Disney princess stories have shaped their understandings of femininity and gender roles?

Population: young women with childhood exposure to Disney princess stories · Method: semi-structured interviews · Fall 2026

I am not assuming Disney definitely shaped every participant. I'm asking participants to reflect on whether and how they experienced that influence. Someone might say Disney strongly shaped their ideas about femininity; someone else might say it had almost no effect. Both responses are useful data.

Chapter III

Where I Stand Epistemologically

Interpretivism

Research that asks what does something mean to the people who experienced it — rather than how much does X cause Y.

This fits because my question is about meaning and interpretation, not measurement. I want participants' own accounts rather than predefined categories, and I don't assume every woman interpreted Disney the same way. There is no single correct effect waiting to be found — there are as many understandings as there are people who watched.

Chapter IV

What This Adds

From representation to interpretation

Most existing research asks what gender roles Disney films portray. My study asks how young women themselves understand those messages. I'm interested in what participants remember, what they think those stories communicated about femininity, and whether they accepted, rejected, or reinterpreted them.

To be precise about the claim: audience interpretation has already been studied elsewhere, so this isn't an empty gap. The contribution is the specific focus — young women aged 18–25, reflecting as adults on their own childhood experience, in their own words.

Chapter V

What We Already Know

Camp 1 — What Disney represented

Litt (2019) examines Cinderella, Sleeping Beauty, Peter Pan, and Alice in Wonderland alongside toys, books, and advertising, showing how 1950s Disney linked gender to romance, family, and growing up. England, Descartes & Collier-Meek (2011), Sex Roles coded gender-role behaviour across the nine classic-era princess films and found consistently stereotyped portrayals — though ones that grew more complex over time.

Camp 2 — The princess changed

Hine, England, Lopreore, Skora Horgan & Hartwell (2018), Social Sciences studied princes and princesses across films released 2009–2016 (The Princess and the Frog, Tangled, Brave, Frozen, Moana) and found princesses displaying an increasingly even mix of masculine and feminine traits — more balanced, "androgynous" profiles than earlier eras. Even popular commentary noticed the shift: the National Organization for Women (2011) described Tangled as Disney moving closer to embracing feminism.

Camp 3 — The audience interprets

Hine, Ivanovic & England (2018) studied 131 children aged 8–9 and how they understood "old" versus "new" princess characters, including Sleeping Beauty and Moana. Representation is not the same as automatic influence — people interpret media through their own experience, which is exactly why this study asks participants for their own interpretations rather than assuming a single effect.

So where is my study? We know what gender roles appear in Disney. We know how the princesses have changed. We know children actively interpret different princesses in the moment of watching. What we know far less about is how the women who grew up with these stories make sense of them looking back, once girlhood is behind them.
Chapter VI

Analytical Framework, Including Positionality

My lens · three sensitising concepts

  • Femininity — what being a "girl" or "woman" means in the stories participants remember.
  • Gender expectations — what the stories suggested women should be like: how they behave, look, love, work, relate to others.
  • Negotiation — whether participants accepted, rejected, reinterpreted, or simply ignored those messages.

These are not hypotheses I am trying to prove — they are sensitising concepts. They tell me where to listen during interviews, not what I will find.

Positionality

I am partly an insider: I belong to the same broad generation as my participants and carry my own experiences with Disney and with ideas about femininity. That can help me understand references and childhood memories, but it also means I might hear my own experience echoed back in participants' answers rather than genuinely new ones. So I need to keep interviews open, listen for difference rather than similarity, and stay alert to moments where I might be nudging an account toward a reading I already hold.

Chapter VII

What I Expect — and Could Be Wrong

  1. Some participants may describe Disney as reinforcing traditional ideas about beauty and romance.
  2. Others may point to newer princesses as offering different, more active models of femininity.
  3. Some may describe a tension between the messages they absorbed as children and what they believe now.

Interpretivism doesn't mean having no expectations — it means staying genuinely open to being wrong. If someone tells me Disney had no meaningful influence at all, that isn't a failed interview. It's still an important finding.

What I Understand — and Don't, Yet

What working with AI helped me understand, and what I still don't understand about my research design

Working through this helped me understand that I can't simply point to Disney's gender representation and conclude it shaped women's identities — representation is not the same thing as influence, and interpretivism means taking participants' own meanings seriously rather than presuming an effect and going looking for it.

What I still haven't settled: how do I tell my participants' own interpretations apart from the shape of my interview questions? And how much can I read into someone's memory of childhood without quietly turning it into proof of causation? That's something I expect I'll only really learn once I'm in the field.
