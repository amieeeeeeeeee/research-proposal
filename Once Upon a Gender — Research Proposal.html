Once Upon a Gender

Disney Princess Stories and Young Women's Understandings of Femininity

An interpretivist study of memory, meaning, and the messages we keep or leave behind

Chapter I

The Question

We are not asking what Disney did to anyone. We are asking what young women make of what they watched.

How do young women aged 18–25 reflect on the ways Disney princess stories shaped their understandings of femininity and gender roles?

The question is written in the past-into-present tense on purpose. It doesn't ask "does Disney cause X." It asks participants to look back, as adults, at something they watched as children, and to tell us — in their own words — what they think it meant. That looking-back is itself the data.

Chapter II

How We Will Look

Epistemological positioning: Interpretivism

This study does not treat femininity as a fixed set of traits that Disney either installs correctly or incorrectly. It treats femininity as something people make sense of — differently, depending on who they are, who they watched with, and what else was happening in their lives at the time. There is no single true effect of a Disney film waiting to be measured; there are as many understandings as there are people who watched.

That is what makes the question interpretivist rather than positivist. We are not testing a hypothesis about how much exposure predicts which attitude. We are generating rich, first-person accounts of meaning-making, through semi-structured interviews, and treating each account as valid data regardless of what it concludes.

If one participant says "Disney taught me that women should be beautiful and find love," and another says "I watched Disney constantly and it had absolutely no effect on me," — both are useful. Neither is the "correct" finding we were looking for.

Our job is not to adjudicate between these two women. It is to understand how each of them arrived at her own reading — what she accepted, what she pushed back on, and what she simply never thought about twice.

Chapter III

Why It Matters

Contribution

Most existing work on Disney princesses and gender falls into one of two camps: studies that analyse what the films contain (content analyses of dialogue, behaviour, appearance), and studies of how children respond to princess media in the moment of watching. What is comparatively rare is asking adults to look back across time — to narrate how, if at all, those early messages travelled with them into how they understand being a woman today.

This study contributes a retrospective, meaning-making account from the audience itself, of one of the most globally distributed sources of gendered storytelling that exists. It does not claim to prove an effect. It aims to describe, in participants' own terms, the different ways early media messages about gender get carried, altered, or set down along the way.

Chapter IV

What We Already Know

Literature review

Two bodies of work set up the puzzle behind this study.

The first examines what Disney princess films actually portray. England, Descartes, and Collier-Meek (2011), in Sex Roles, coded the behavioural characteristics of prince and princess characters across the nine classic-era Disney Princess films. They found consistently stereotyped gender portrayals throughout — but also found that those portrayals grew more complex over time, with no straightforward, linear march toward equality. Even the most recent film they studied, The Princess and the Frog, still carried some conventional gender scripting alongside its more modern touches.

The second body of work looks at audiences, not texts. Anna Zsubori's research with tween audiences in Hungary is especially useful here, because rather than asking what princess films say, it asks what children themselves do with the idea of "the princess" — how they actively interpret, question, and locally reinterpret it, shaped by their own cultural and social context, rather than simply receiving it. Her work is a reminder that audiences are never blank pages.

We know a great deal about what Disney princesses represent. We know children are active interpreters, not passive recipients, in the moment of watching. What we know far less about is how the girls who grew up watching them make sense of those messages later — once girlhood is behind them and femininity is something they are now living, not just watching.

That gap is where this study sits: content research tells us what was said; audience research with children tells us it wasn't absorbed uncritically. This study asks what happened to those messages afterwards — in memory, in adulthood, in the process of becoming a woman rather than watching one on screen.

Chapter V

Analytical Framework, Including Positionality

Gender socialization and meaning-making

The study is oriented by gender socialization theory — the idea that people learn cultural scripts for gendered behaviour partly through the media they consume as children — read together with a meaning-making, audience-reception lens that treats viewers as active interpreters rather than passive recipients of those scripts. Three sensitising concepts, rather than fixed hypotheses, guide the interview design and the analysis:

  • Femininity — what participants understand being a "girl" or "woman" to mean, in their own words.
  • Gender expectations — ideas participants encountered about how women are expected to behave, look, love, work, or relate to others.
  • Negotiation — the ways participants accepted, rejected, reinterpreted, or simply ignored the messages Disney offered them.

Sensitising concepts are used deliberately instead of hypotheses. They orient attention during interviews and coding without predicting an outcome — they tell us where to listen, not what we expect to hear.

Positionality

I come to this project as someone who grew up watching Disney princess films myself, which is part of why the question interests me — but it also means I carry my own memories and assumptions about what these films "mean," which could quietly shape how I hear participants during interviews or how I read their transcripts afterwards. I try to manage this by treating my own reading of Disney as one more interpretation among many, not the benchmark participants' answers are checked against, and by staying alert — in the interview and in analysis — to moments where I might be nudging an account toward a reading I already hold. I note these moments as they come up, rather than assuming I can remove myself from the analysis entirely.

A Note on Working with AI

I used AI as a thinking and building partner, not as an author. For the proposal, I brought my own research question, sensitising concepts, and sources, and used AI to pressure-test whether the design was genuinely interpretivist and to sharpen the wording of the puzzle. For this page, I described the assignment and the content, and AI drafted the HTML and design and talked me through publishing it — I did not know how to code this myself beforehand. Every claim, source, and concept on this page is one I can explain and defend without it.

What I Understand — and Don't, Yet

What working with AI helped me understand, and what I still don't understand about my research design

Working through this with AI helped me understand, more clearly than reading about it in the abstract, why an interpretivist question has to be written the way it's written — that phrases like "how do participants reflect on" are doing real epistemological work, not just softer wording, and that a positivist version of this question would have looked completely different. It also helped me see positionality as an ongoing practice rather than a single paragraph you write once and file away.

What I'm still not fully settled on is how much of what a participant tells me in an interview, years after childhood, is really "what Disney did" versus a story she's since built about her own past — and whether that distinction even matters for a study like this one, or whether it's a false distinction I'm still trying to let go of.
